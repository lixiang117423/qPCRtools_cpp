\subsection{Typical analysis workflow}

To illustrate a typical analysis, we analyzed the bundled example dataset
(\texttt{examples/cq.csv} and \texttt{examples/design.csv}), comprising the
target gene \textit{fos-glo-myc} normalized against the reference gene
\textit{Beta Actin} across three treatment groups (0, 0.5, 1) with three
biological replicates each. Using the $\Delta\Delta C_t$ method with a
Welch two-sample $t$-test against the control group~(0), relative expression
rose monotonically to 1.11 at dose~0.5 and 1.59 at dose~1, both significantly
different from the control ($p = 0.006$ and $p = 0.022$;
Figure~\ref{fig:results}). Following the R implementation, expression values
are normalized to the mean $\Delta C_t$ over all samples rather than to the
control group, so the control group is not forced to exactly~1
(here $0.59 \pm 0.12$). The group means, standard deviations and $p$-values
quoted here are reproduced without any dependency on the application or on
the R package by \texttt{validation/check\_example\_stats.R}.
Applied to a four-fold dilution
series of four genes, the standard-curve module recovered slopes between
$-2.07$ and $-2.24$ with $R^2 > 0.99$ for three of the four primers
(efficiencies 0.85--0.95), illustrating amplification-efficiency estimation
that, per MIQE guidance, determines which quantification method is
appropriate. The complete workflow---import, configuration, analysis, and
export---is performed through the graphical interface without writing code.

\subsection{Equivalence to the R package}

{\sloppy We validated the C++ implementation against the original R package
\cite{li2022qpcrtools} by running both on identical inputs for every shared
method---$\Delta\Delta C_t$ (\texttt{CalExp2ddCt}), the standard curve
(\texttt{CalCurve}), and the efficiency-based RqPCR method
(\texttt{CalExpRqPCR})---and comparing every resulting quantity pairwise
(Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}, Figure~\ref{fig:equivalence}). Inputs were
drawn from the repository's example data and from a dilution series used in
the original R package; both implementations read the same on-disk files, so
any difference reflects the implementations alone.\par}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Equivalence of C++ and R results across all shared methods on
identical inputs. Relative difference is $|R - \mathrm{C{+}{+}}|/|R|$.}
\label{tab:equivalence}
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
Method & Compared quantities & Pairs & Max.\ rel.\ diff.\ \\
\midrule
$\Delta\Delta C_t$  & expression, SD                          & 6  & $3.8 \times 10^{-15}$ \\
Standard curve      & slope, intercept, $R^2$, efficiency     & 16 & $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$  \\
RqPCR               & expression, SE                          & 16 & $2.1 \times 10^{-15}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For the two expression methods, $\Delta\Delta C_t$ and RqPCR, every compared
quantity---expression values, standard deviations, and standard
errors---matched between implementations to within floating-point precision
(maximum relative difference $< 4 \times 10^{-15}$). For the standard curve,
slope, intercept, $R^2$, and efficiency agreed to within $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$
relative difference; the residual is attributable to the R package rounding
its tabulated output to two to four decimal places, since the underlying
$C_q$ inputs are identical. Plotting every C++ result against its R
counterpart, all points fall on the line $y = x$
(Figure~\ref{fig:equivalence}). These results confirm that the C++
reimplementation preserves the analytical behavior of the R package across
all shared methods. The validation is fully reproducible from the open
scripts in \texttt{validation/} (see Availability).
